# Chapter 10 — Rotation, Waves, and Planetary Weather

## 10.1 Compare inertia with the Coriolis effect

In a frame rotating with angular velocity \(\boldsymbol\Omega\), moving gas experiences Coriolis acceleration \(-2\boldsymbol\Omega\times\mathbf u\). In a local horizontal model away from the equator, its strength is characterized by \(f=2\Omega\sin\varphi\), where \(\varphi\) is latitude. Comparing advective acceleration \(U^2/L\) with Coriolis acceleration \(|f|U\) gives the Rossby number

\[
\mathrm{Ro}=\frac{U}{|f|L}.
\]

At small Rossby number, pressure gradients can balance Coriolis acceleration so that the steady horizontal wind flows approximately along pressure contours. Near the equator, \(f\) approaches zero, and the change in \(f\) with latitude matters for wave motion. [Showman & Polvani (2011)](https://doi.org/10.1088/0004-637X/738/1/71)

**Worked example.** For a synchronously rotating planet with a three-day period, \(f\approx3.4\times10^{-5}\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}\) at \(45^\circ\). With wind speed \(1\,000\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\) and scale \(10^7\) m, \(\mathrm{Ro}\approx3\). Advective acceleration can be comparable to or larger than the Coriolis term at those scales, so assuming a geostrophic balance would be a poor starting point.

## 10.2 Connect circulation to a phase curve

A synchronously rotating planet receives steady heating on one hemisphere. Compare an advection timescale \(L/U\) with the time over which radiation significantly changes an air parcel’s temperature. If radiation acts quickly, temperature can respond mainly to local heating. If winds redistribute heat before it is radiated, they can shift the brightest region away from the substellar point. Waves also transport momentum. In idealized models, a combination of equatorial Kelvin and Rossby waves driven by day–night heating can accelerate an eastward equatorial jet. [Showman & Polvani (2011)](https://doi.org/10.1088/0004-637X/738/1/71)

An infrared phase curve measures changing disk-integrated brightness during the orbit. Its peak can constrain longitudinal brightness patterns. The interpretation depends on wavelength, pressure, clouds, and viewing geometry, so compare observations with synthetic phase curves computed from the circulation rather than identifying the peak offset directly with a wind speed.
