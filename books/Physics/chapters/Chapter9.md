# Chapter 9 — Wave Optics, Telescopes, and Spectrographs

## 9.1 When two points blur together

Light from a distant point source spreads into a diffraction pattern at a telescope focus. For an ideal unobstructed circular aperture of diameter \(D\), the angular separation at the Rayleigh criterion is approximately \(1.22\lambda/D\) radians. Longer wavelengths are harder to resolve at the same aperture. Collecting area and angular resolution are distinct quantities, even though both depend on diameter. [OpenStax — Circular Apertures and Resolution](https://openstax.org/books/university-physics-volume-3/pages/4-5-circular-apertures-and-resolution)

**Worked example.** A planet at a projected separation of 1 AU around a star 10 parsecs away appears 0.1 arcseconds from the star. At 550 nm a 6 m aperture gives a Rayleigh angle of about 0.023 arcseconds. The separation exceeds that limit, but detecting the planet still requires suppressing stellar glare. For a coronagraph, both its inner working angle and its achievable contrast at the planet’s separation matter. [NASA — Habitable Worlds Observatory](https://science.nasa.gov/astrophysics/programs/habitable-worlds-observatory/)

## 9.2 Measure a shift smaller than a resolution element

A spectrograph’s resolving power is \(R=\lambda/\Delta\lambda\). The classical Doppler relation for small line-of-sight velocities is \(\delta\lambda/\lambda\simeq v_r/c\). At \(R=100{,}000\), one resolution element spans about 3 km/s. A 1 m/s shift is only about one three-thousandth of that width. The position of a well-measured profile can be estimated to a fraction of its width, especially by combining information from many lines. Photon noise, wavelength calibration, and changes in stellar line shapes then set practical limits on radial-velocity precision. [Exoplanets — The Radial Velocity Method](#Exoplanets/Chapter5)
